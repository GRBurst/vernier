# Parser timing sample

The workshop opened late. Nobody minded, because the coffee was good and the room was warm after a cold walk from the station. Mara had brought a box of printed agendas, but most people read theirs on a phone and left the paper untouched.

The first talk explained why the city had changed its recycling rules twice in a single year. Residents who had learned the old sorting scheme were confused when glass, which had gone into the green bin since the nineties, suddenly belonged in a separate crate. The speaker admitted that the council should have tested the new leaflets with a few households before printing forty thousand of them. Questions followed. One man asked whether the crates would be collected in winter, and the answer was yes, weekly, except in the week between the holidays.

The proposal, which the executive committee rejected after extensive deliberation, caused significant delays. It had asked for a shared tool library in every district, staffed by volunteers and funded by a small fee on each loan. Critics argued that the fee would keep away exactly the people who needed the tools most. Supporters replied that a library without any income would close within two years, as the last one had. Both sides had a point.

After lunch the group split into three tables. The first table sketched a map of the neighbourhood and marked every place where a bench, a tap or a public toilet was missing. The second table read through complaints that the housing office had received about damp walls, broken lifts and heating that switched off at night. The third table, which had the fewest members but the loudest voices, argued about whether the library should lend ladders at all.

Dr. Okafor, who had run a similar scheme in another town, joined the third table for half an hour. She said that ladders were the most borrowed item in her library and the least often damaged. Insurance had been the real obstacle. Once the town agreed to cover borrowers under its existing policy, the question of liability, which had stalled the project for months, simply disappeared.

It rained. The coffee ran out at three. The windows fogged up, and someone opened the back door to let the steam out.

By four o'clock the tables had written their findings on large sheets of paper and taped them to the wall. The map showed eleven missing benches, four broken taps and not a single public toilet north of the river. The complaints fell into a clear pattern: the buildings that had been renovated most recently had the most problems, which suggested that the contractor, rather than the tenants, was to blame. That conclusion surprised the housing officer, who had assumed the opposite for years.

What should happen next? Mara proposed that each table choose one concrete step that could be finished before the next meeting. The map table would photograph the missing benches and send the pictures to the parks department with a short, polite letter. The complaints table would ask the housing office for the contractor's name and the dates of every repair it had carried out since the renovation. The library table would draft a one-page budget, including the insurance, and circulate it by email.

Not everyone was convinced. An older woman near the door said that she had attended meetings like this one for thirty years and that the benches were still missing. Mara thanked her and asked whether she would be willing to check, in a month, whether the letter had been answered. She agreed, to general laughter.

The meeting closed at half past five. Chairs were stacked, cups were washed and the sheets came down from the wall, rolled up and carried out under someone's arm. Outside, the rain had stopped, the streetlights were on, and the pavement shone. A bus came almost at once.

Looking back, the day did better than most of its kind, not because anyone made a decision that a council could act on, but because three small groups of neighbours, who had until then known each other only by sight, left with a task they believed they could finish, a date by which to finish it, and the names of the people who would check. Whether that feeling lasts is another matter. Meetings of this sort often end in warm agreement and then fade, as the letters go unanswered, the volunteers find other things to do and the sheets of paper, carefully rolled up at the end of the day, gather dust in a cupboard that nobody opens. Still, the benches are countable. So are the taps.

The next meeting is planned for the first Saturday of the following month, in the same room, with the same coffee. Anyone who cannot attend but wants to help is asked to write to the address on the back of the agenda. Children are welcome. Please bring a pencil, a sense of humour and, if you own one, a ladder that you would be willing to lend to a stranger for a weekend without asking for a deposit, a signature or a promise to bring it back clean.
